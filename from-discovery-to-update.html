From Discovery to Update: What Happens After a Software Vulnerability Is Found?

A researcher discovers a serious software vulnerability. What happens next? Who fixes it, and how does that fix reach the people using the software?

In my previous post, I discussed the PHP vulnerabilities collectively known as “PHP2Shell”. That story led me to another question: what happens between discovering a security flaw and getting a fix into the hands of the people affected? In this post, I’ll explore the main stages of that process, from discovering and reporting a vulnerability to preparing a patch and installing the update.

CASE STUDY WORKFLOW: 'PHP2SHELL'
Tracing CVE-2026-109618 & CVE-2026-109619 from initial discovery to coordinated patch release.

1. Discovering the Vulnerability

In any sort of problem solving, problem identification remains the first and most crucial step. For any problem to be patched for the long term, narrowing down the root cause of the vulnerability is key. It is also important to identify false positives, which may occur when someone notices something that looks like a security vulnerability but isn’t one.

In the PHP2Shell case, Calif’s research timeline dates the beginning of its discovery work to August 1, 2026. The researchers investigated how memory-limit failures could interrupt PHP’s normal processing and leave internal data in an unsafe state, eventually identifying multiple paths to remote code execution.

2. Reporting the Vulnerability

Once a potential vulnerability is identified, the next step is to report the vulnerability to the software developers or security team using proper reporting channels (such as its security email address or vulnerability reporting form). The report should include core details of the vulnerability such as the description of the issue, where the issue is located, its possible impact if misused, steps to reproduce the issue (exact description of the sequence of actions that led to the issue, in detail and screenshots) where possible, and optionally, the reporter may also include a suggested process for preparing a fix for the problem if they have the technical knowledge to provide them. This is the phase where sensitive information is shared on a need-to-know basis to avoid unwanted public disclosure that may lead to the misuse of the vulnerability before the issue is fixed. The aim is not to hide the issue permanently, but to coordinate public disclosure so that the responsible team has a reasonable opportunity to investigate and address it, while reducing the risk of exploitation.

According to Calif's timeline, Ryan Austin reported the first bug to PHP's security team on August 11, 2026, beginning the coordinated disclosure process. Calif shared a remote code execution proof of concept with PHP on August 14, and PHP confirmed the next day that it worked as expected. A second bug was reported on August 21, 2026.

3. Preparation and Test of the Fix

After recognizing the core issue and its possible impacts, the software developers or security team begin working on fixing the root cause of the vulnerability. The fix may take the form of a security patch or a software update. Before releasing the fix, however, the development and testing teams, sometimes with help from the initial reporter, test whether it addresses the vulnerability. They also check whether it unexpectedly affects the software’s other functions. However, some vulnerabilities are more complicated than others, and in such scenarios, if the complete fix isn't immediately available, the team may recommend temporary workarounds to reduce the risk, though such measures shouldn't be taken as a long-term resolution of the vulnerability. Once the fix has been tested, it can be released with guidance explaining which versions are affected and what users need to update.

On October 21, 2026, PHP released versions 8.5.12, 8.4.27, 8.3.36 and 8.2.35 to address the vulnerabilities. An advisory also described temporary measures for organizations that could not update immediately. However, PHP explicitly warned that these measures were only temporary workarounds and did not make an unpatched installation safe.

4. Installation and Verification of the Update

When a patch is released, it doesn't mean that every affected system is automatically protected against said vulnerability. Some updates are installed automatically, some are made mandatory by the software provider, and some may require the users, website owners, or the IT administrators to take certain action (such as downloading the patch from a trusted source, or manually clicking 'Update' inside the software). Even an automatic or mandatory update may require additional steps such as restarting the software or system, or granting a required permission. The people responsible for affected systems need to follow the provider’s instructions, apply the appropriate update or patch, and verify that it was successfully installed and has taken effect. They should also perform basic checks to ensure that the software’s expected functions still work. The key principle is that a patch being available does not, by itself, mean that every affected system has been patched.

PHP provided the patches in advance to Linux distributions and long-term support vendors, and urged users to update immediately. Website operators therefore needed to obtain the appropriate update for their installation, either from PHP or through their vendor. Some vendors also provided backported fixes to older versions of PHP.

As a +2 Management student in Kathmandu studying Computer Science, learning about the discovery of PHP2Shell initially made me curious on how cybersecurity vulnerabilities are discovered, how they impact the affected systems, and how they are resolved step-by-step.
